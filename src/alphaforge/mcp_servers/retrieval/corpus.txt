Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) is the empirical observation that stock
prices continue to move in the direction of an earnings surprise for several
weeks after the announcement. Ball and Brown (1968) first documented the effect.
Explanations include underreaction to new information and limited investor
attention. The drift is strongest for high-surprise stocks and weakens after
public disclosure of the anomaly.

Short-term reversal refers to the tendency of stocks with extreme negative
short-term returns to bounce back over the following days. Jegadeesh (1990)
documented 1-month reversal in US equities. Liquidity provision by
market-makers and temporary order-flow pressure are common explanations.

Volatility regimes matter for expected returns: high realized volatility is
associated with elevated risk premiums but also with poorer risk-adjusted
returns for leveraged strategies. Persistence in volatility (volatility
clustering, Engle 1982) makes percentile-based regime classification stable
out of sample.

Cross-sectional and time-series momentum: assets with strong past 3-12 month
performance tend to continue outperforming over the next 1-12 months
(Jegadeesh and Titman 1993). Momentum crashes can occur at market reversals,
so risk management is essential when harvesting momentum.

Volume shocks: abnormal trading volume often signals information arrival or
institutional rebalancing. Stocks with extreme volume spikes tend to
underperform over the following week, consistent with temporary price pressure
and subsequent reversal (Llorente et al. 2002; Gabaix et al. 2006).

Seasonalities: the Monday effect denotes historically lower average returns on
Mondays relative to other weekdays, most notably Fridays. The effect has
weakened since the 1990s but remains a standard sanity-check seasonality in
event studies.

Transaction costs: realistic round-trip costs for liquid US equities range
from 5 to 15 basis points including slippage and fees. Any event-study
backtest that ignores costs overstates strategy viability, especially for
high-turnover event-driven signals.

Multiple-testing discipline: when testing many hypotheses simultaneously, the
expected number of false discoveries grows with the number of tests. The
Bonferroni correction controls the family-wise error rate by testing each
hypothesis at alpha/m; the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the expected
false discovery rate (FDR) at level q. Reporting both is standard practice in
quantitative research.

Human-in-the-loop review in agentic research systems: automated pipelines can
propose and test hypotheses at scale, but checkpoint reviews before capital
allocation are critical. A checkpoint after statistical validation and before
backtesting lets a researcher reject technically significant but
economically implausible signals.
